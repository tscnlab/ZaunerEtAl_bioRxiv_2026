# Evidence-supported positioning

## Directly established within the bounded evidence

Objective personal-light patterns have been associated with sleep timing and sleepiness, psychiatric outcomes, incident type 2 diabetes, incident cardiovascular disease, obesity-related outcomes and mortality in observational population studies. The largest verified health cohorts measured broad-spectrum light at the wrist for approximately one week. Smaller studies used wrist or bedside measurements in older adults.

## Methods and contextual support

The Brown recommendations supply physiology-based melanopic contexts for daytime, pre-sleep and sleep-environment exposure. Wallace et al. demonstrates that large population samples show the expected 24-hour rhythm and geographic, seasonal and demographic patterning, while also illustrating the limitations of wrist photopic exposure for an ocular melanopic claim.

## What remains uncertain

The evidence does not provide a causal personal-light risk function, a direct retinal dose, or an international harmonised near-eye baseline. It also does not show that one week of exposure represents long-term personal exposure for every outcome.

## Exact gap addressed by the Nature Health manuscript

The manuscript establishes where near-eye melanopic exposure lies across local clock time and recommendation contexts, and separates shared time, site, participant, participant-day and immediate-context contributions under one harmonised international protocol. That architecture is needed to interpret, design and transport health-linked exposure studies, but it does not itself estimate a health association.

## Strongest defensible positioning statement

Population studies make personal light exposure health relevant, while this study supplies the international near-eye exposure architecture needed to understand what those epidemiological exposure summaries represent in everyday life.
