402 The expected 24-hour rhythm was evident across nine sites in seven countries. The
403 advance was a multiscale account of this everyday exposome component: amplitude across
404 sites, people and days, and its reorganisation across immediate settings. The shared local-
405 clock pattern accounted for the largest share of full-model R² in the daily-pattern analysis.
406 Separate hourly decompositions showed large light-source and immediate-setting contrasts,
407 with the next-largest shares of variation in fitted hourly patterns after time of day. Participant
408 patterns and participant-day shifts together exceeded site patterns in the daily-pattern
409 model, while site, photoperiod and measured person-level associations were metric-specific.
410 Different outcomes and decompositions make this a qualitative synthesis rather than a single
411 quantitative ranking.

412 The consensus recommendations provide the clearest health-relevant benchmark. Only
413 24.0% of waking minutes reached the daytime range, while 63.3% of pre-sleep and 87.7% of
414 bedside sleep-environment minutes met their upper limits. Modelled free-day adherence was
415 lower during daytime and preceding sleep, varying by site. Pre-sleep adherence was higher
416 but inconclusive under stricter coverage; temporal dependence remained unresolved.
417 Outdoor exposure exceeded the daytime recommendation, but all other conditions remained
418 below it on average. The fitted sleep-category mean exceeded the bedside limit, while
419 87.7% of pooled bedside sleep-environment minutes met it. These quantities describe
420 different aspects of the exposure distribution and should be interpreted together. These
421 pooled fractions, recommendation-window proportions and hourly means are distinct
422 estimands. Together, they identify intervention opportunities and shortfalls. The
423 recommendations principally address healthy adults aged 18 to 55 with regular daytime
424 schedules8.

425 This baseline connects mechanistic recommendations with population evidence. Large wrist-
426 sensor cohorts have associated day and night light patterns with psychiatric disorders,
427 incident type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and mortality, and light timing with sleep
428 timing and sleepiness10–15. Other cohorts have linked evening, nocturnal or strongly seasonal
429 light with sleep in bipolar disorder, obesity, diabetes, hypertension and metabolic health16–19.
430 These outcomes span sleep and major non-communicable diseases. The exposome frames
431 environmental contributions across the life course20,21 but rarely includes time-resolved
432 ocular light. This evidence supports personal-light measurement, not clinical risk
