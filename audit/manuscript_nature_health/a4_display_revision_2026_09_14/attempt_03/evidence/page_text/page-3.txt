48 Introduction

49 Light reaching the eye supports vision and signals circadian timing, sleep, alertness and
50 neuroendocrine responses1–5. Melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance (melanopic EDI)
51 weights illuminance for melanopsin-related sensitivity and is most relevant to these effects6,7.
52 Expert consensus recommends at least 250 lx melanopic EDI at the eye during daytime, no
53 more than 10 lx during the three hours before sleep and no more than 1 lx in the sleep
54 environment for healthy adults with regular daytime schedules8. These physiology-based
55 ranges make daily light a health-relevant exposure, but are not individual risk functions.

56 Population studies and reviews associate personal light patterns with sleep and major non-
57 communicable diseases9. Wrist-sensor studies link light timing and day-night distribution with
58 sleep timing and sleepiness, psychiatric disorders, incident type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular
59 disease and mortality10–15. Other cohorts link evening, nocturnal or seasonal light with sleep
60 in bipolar disorder and with obesity, diabetes, hypertension and metabolic health16–19. The
61 exposome framework characterises non-genetic exposures across the life course20,21, yet
62 rarely includes time-resolved ocular melanopic light, the everyday component examined
63 here. These studies establish population relevance but mostly measured broad-spectrum
64 wrist or bedside light, providing only proxy evidence for melanopic ocular exposure.

65 An everyday exposure baseline against these recommendations is missing. Personal
66 exposure to natural and electric light follows an expected 24-hour rhythm, including in large
67 wrist-sensor cohorts22, but previous studies focus on particular populations, occupations or
68 settings23–25. Its amplitude and timing across sites and countries, and the contributions of
69 time, site, individuals, day-to-day variation and immediate context, remain uncertain.
70 Daylight availability, satellite estimates and fixed-site measurements cannot resolve these
71 layers as people move through buildings, transport, outdoor spaces and sleep
72 environments26. Sensor position also matters: near-eye sensing is closer to the incident
73 ocular field but is not a retinal measurement; wrist and chest sensors capture related fields
74 shaped by posture, wear and occlusion27–33.
